# Tarka Sabha Strategy 2026–2031

**Status:** Working strategy. Review on the first Monday of every month.
**Date:** 20 October 2026

**Operating plan:** See `MASTER_PLAN.md` for the phase-by-phase execution plan.
**Decision:** Continue, but reposition the product. The “multi-agent debate” is not the business. The defensible product is the **review and decision record for technical work**.

**Sharpened wedge:** See `OPPORTUNITY.md` — Decision Debt Control for AI-written code, the narrow category we can launch first.

---

## 1. Verdict: will this still matter in five years?

**Yes — with high confidence in the problem, medium confidence in the category, and low confidence in the current product form.**

The need to explain and defend important decisions is durable. It predates AI and will outlast any particular model. What is not durable is a generic “council of agents” chat. Models will get better, cheaper, and more capable of role-play. The orchestration will be copied or bundled.

The five-year opportunity is:

> **Tarka Sabha is the decision record for engineering and product teams.** It runs a structured review of a design doc, PRD, incident, or vendor choice; links claims to evidence; records dissent; and produces an approval-ready decision memo that can be defended months later.

The product must move from *conversation* to *accountable decision*. If it does, it is relevant through 2031. If it stays a debate toy, it is a 2026 novelty.

**Confidence by claim**

| Claim | Confidence | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Teams will still need to explain why a decision was made | High | Audit, onboarding, incidents, and regulation all depend on it |
| AI will be embedded in more decisions, not fewer | High | Agent adoption and governance spending are both rising |
| Buyers will pay for evidence and accountability | Medium-high | Governance budgets exist, but the category is still forming |
| “Multi-agent council” remains a differentiator | Low | Orchestration is easy to copy and may not beat a single model |
| Tarka can become a system of record | Medium | Requires workflow, integrations, trust, and proprietary outcome data |

## 3. Why now: the evidence

### 3.1 The agent market is growing, but the correction is the opening
Agent forecasts vary, which is normal for a young category. BCC Research projected the AI agents market growing from $8B in 2025 to $48.3B by 2030 [1]. MarketsandMarkets projected $7.84B in 2025 to $52.62B by 2030 [2]. Treat the exact numbers as directional, not precise.

More important is the correction. Gartner expects **over 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by the end of 2027** because of cost, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls [3]. Gartner also estimated that only about 130 of thousands of agentic vendors are real [3].

That is not a reason to leave the space. It is the reason to sell **proof, control, and accountable outcomes** instead of autonomous agents.

### 3.2 Decision intelligence is becoming a real budget line
Decision intelligence forecasts point in the same direction:
- MarketsandMarkets: $13.3B in 2024 to $50.1B by 2030 [4].
- SNS Insider: $17B in 2025 to $88.98B by 2035, with decision support the largest segment [5].

Again, the numbers vary. The directional signal is consistent: enterprises are funding systems that improve and govern decisions, not just content generation.

### 3.3 Accountability is becoming an explicit executive responsibility
Gartner predicts that **by 2030, 80% of Global 500 companies will contractually designate their CIO or chief AI officer as the “evidence custodian” for AI accountability** [6]. The evidence trail must show:
- what data the AI accessed
- what instructions shaped the output
- which tools it used
- what actions it took
- where human approval was required
- who owned the outcome

That is almost exactly Tarka’s review record. The difference is that we currently record a conversation, not a governed decision.

TechTarget’s analysis calls this the shift from an **audit trail** to a **decision trail**: what evidence was available, what the AI produced, what the human reviewed, and who owned the final decision [7].

### 3.4 Governance spending is already large
IDC reports that enterprises allocate an average of **16.7% of planned AI spending to AI and agent security and governance** [8]. IDC also predicts that by 2030, up to 20% of G1000 organisations will face lawsuits, fines, or CIO dismissals from inadequate agent controls [8].

Governance is no longer a compliance afterthought. It is a budget line. But governance alone is crowded. Tarka should be the **decision layer** that produces the evidence governance teams need.

### 3.5 AI is moving up the software lifecycle
Uber now uses AI for a first-pass PRD review. DoorDash uses AI code review. Cloudflare uses specialised agents for security, performance, and correctness checks. Cloudflare reports that tightly scoped specialised agents outperform a single general-purpose reviewer [9].

This validates the review-board model — but only when each reviewer has a narrow job and the output is integrated into the existing workflow. It also warns us: the value is in **review quality**, not in the number of agents.

### 3.6 Multi-agent systems are not automatically better
Research is clear that coordination can destroy value. A 2026 decision-theory paper shows that, without new exogenous signals, a delegated multi-agent network is dominated by a centralised Bayes decision maker with the same information [10]. Another 2026 paper notes that single-agent setups often outperform multi-agent versions and that failures come from coordination, orchestration, and workflow design [11].

**Implication:** We cannot win by claiming “more agents = better.” We win by adding things a single model does not have:
- independent reviewers with explicit roles
- tools and source evidence
- a scoring rubric
- recorded dissent
- human approval
- an auditable decision trail
- outcome tracking

If the council does not add those, remove it.

### 3.7 Buyers have a proof problem
Seventy-four percent of major enterprises now run at least one AI solution in production, but roughly half cannot consistently measure ROI [12]. That is the opening for a product that produces a specific, reviewable artifact and records whether the decision worked.

## 5. Competitive reality

This is not white space. Adjacent and direct competitors already exist:

- **Decision Log** sells “one decision record for people and agents,” with evidence, immutability, supersession, review, API, CLI, and MCP [13]. This is the closest direct competitor. We must not be a generic decision log.
- **Cloverpop** sells enterprise decision intelligence with a decision system of record, human-plus-agent workflows, governance, and outcome learning [14].
- **SAS, IBM, and other enterprise platforms** sell governed decision intelligence and AI control layers to large enterprises [15][16].
- **ChatPRD** owns the PRD drafting/review workflow for product teams, with templates and integrations [17].
- **AlphaSense** proves that decision-grade intelligence can support a large business: it reported more than $700M ARR and 7,800 customers in Q2 2026 [18].

### Our defensible wedge
Do not compete with generic decision intelligence or PRD drafting. Own:

> **The technical decision review record for engineering teams** — architecture, technical design, vendor selection, incident postmortem, and AI-system review.

Why this wedge:
- The buyer is identifiable: CTO, head of engineering, staff engineer, principal architect.
- The pain is expensive and recurring.
- The artifacts already exist: design docs, ADRs, PRDs, incident reports.
- The workflow is measurable: review time, risks caught, decisions changed.
- Incumbents are broad; we can be sharper and faster.
- Engineering teams already accept review workflows (code review, design review, security review).

## 6. The product we will build

### 6.1 Product model

**Review → Evidence → Decision → Approval → Outcome**

The core object is not a chat. It is a **Review** with:
- title, type, owner, status
- context and constraints
- options considered
- reviewers and their roles
- claims and evidence links
- dissent and unresolved questions
- recommendation
- decision owner and approver
- approval timestamp
- revisit date
- outcome notes

### 6.2 The five surfaces

1. **Review board**
   Specialised reviewers, not fantasy characters. Default roles:
   - Chair / synthesizer
   - Researcher / evidence
   - Critic / failure modes
   - Domain reviewer (security, data, product, operations)
   - Devil’s advocate
   Names are optional; roles are the product.

2. **Evidence ledger**
   Every material claim has a source, date, quote, confidence, and status:
   - verified
   - disputed
   - unsupported
   Human reviewers can confirm or reject. Critical unsupported claims can block approval.

3. **Decision memo**
   A client-ready, one-page decision record:
   - context
   - recommendation
   - alternatives
   - risks and mitigations
   - dissent
   - evidence
   - assumptions and revisit triggers
   - owner and approval

4. **Decision log**
   Searchable institutional memory:
   - what we decided
   - why
   - what we knew then
   - what happened
   - what we would do differently

5. **Integrations**
   - GitHub: ADRs, pull requests, design docs
   - Notion / Confluence: source docs and published memo
   - Linear / Jira: follow-up actions
   - Slack / Teams: review requests and approvals
   - API / MCP: let agents read and write decision records

### 6.3 What to cut or hide

- Remove “Debate” from the primary navigation and marketing. Keep it as an internal experiment only.
- Stop leading with “council.” Lead with the decision type.
- Do not require a key per agent. Use one workspace key, managed credits, or BYOK in advanced settings.
- Do not expose raw transcripts by default when sharing. Share the memo; make the transcript an audit appendix.
- Do not build a marketplace, mobile app, or custom agent builder before paid retention.
- Do not optimise for agent count. Optimise for review quality and time-to-decision.

### 6.4 What must be true technically

- Durable background jobs, streaming, retries, cancellation, and provider fallbacks
- Per-run cost ledger and hard spend caps
- Source snapshots, citations, and semantic evidence verification
- Private-by-default data, retention controls, redaction, expiring links
- Audit events for every approval and state change
- Team workspaces, roles, comments, and approvals
- Committed E2E tests and observability before enterprise sales

### Beachhead
**Architecture and technical design review for 20–200 person engineering teams.**

### Buyer
- CTO / VP Engineering / Head of Engineering
- Staff or principal engineer who owns architecture review
- Fractional CTO or consultancy delivering architecture reviews

### Trigger events
- Before starting a major build or migration
- Before adopting a new vendor or AI model
- After an incident
- During due diligence
- Before a board or leadership decision

### Job to be done
“Give me a rigorous review of this design, show me what we are missing, and give me a record I can share with the team and defend later.”

### Why they pay
- A bad architecture decision costs weeks or months.
- A consultant review costs $5k–$50k.
- A security or compliance finding can cost far more.
- The decision record saves time in onboarding, audits, and future reviews.

### Packaging to test

|---|---|---|
| Free | $0 | One review, BYOK, public output |
| Pro | $29/month | Private reviews, exports, history, templates |

| Per review | $49–199 | High-value architecture, diligence, or incident review |
| Enterprise | $15k–50k/year | SSO, DPA, retention, audit log, dedicated support |

### Managed models
- Offer managed credits for the free trial and paid plans.
- Keep BYOK as a cost-saving option for technical users.
- Route cheap models for extraction and classification; use stronger models for judgment.
- Enforce hard spend caps per workspace and per review.
- Target gross margin above 70% after model costs.

### Pricing principle
Charge for the **decision record and review workflow**, not for tokens. Tokens are an input cost, not a value metric.

## 9. Go-to-market

### 9.1 Land with one artifact
“Architecture review in 30 minutes.”
- Upload a design doc or link a GitHub ADR.
- Tarka returns a review memo with risks, dissent, evidence, and recommended changes.
- The team comments, approves, and shares the record.
- The record becomes the reason to come back for the next decision.

### 9.2 First 90 days
**Weeks 1–2**
- Rewrite homepage, pricing, and onboarding around “decision review.”
- Hide debate mode.
- Rename default roles to professional roles.
- Define the Review, Evidence, Decision, and Approval schema.
- Recruit 10 design partners from engineering networks and consultancies.

**Weeks 3–6**
- Ship the decision memo v2 and evidence ledger.
- Add private-by-default reviews, redaction, and expiring share links.
- Add a hosted trial with managed credits and spend caps.
- Run 20 real reviews. Record time saved, risks caught, and decisions changed.

**Weeks 7–12**
- Publish a benchmark: council vs single model vs human review on 30–50 real tasks.
- Publish 3 case studies with before/after decisions.
- Add Stripe, team invites, and approval flow.
- Convert 5 design partners to paid pilots.
- Add a GitHub App or ADR integration.

### 9.3 Channels
- GitHub App and ADR templates
- SEO: “architecture review checklist,” “ADR review,” “PRD review template,” “incident postmortem review”
- Open benchmark and methodology
- Partnerships with fractional CTOs, dev agencies, and VC platform teams
- LinkedIn case studies aimed at engineering leaders
- Conference talks on decision quality and AI governance

### 9.4 Sales motion
Product-led trial → team invite → paid pilot → annual team plan → enterprise.
Do not hire a sales team until there are 20+ paying teams and a repeatable playbook.

## 12. Risks and kill criteria

### Risk: models become good enough that a single prompt is enough
**Mitigation:** The product is the record, evidence, and approval workflow. Even a perfect model needs provenance and accountability.

### Risk: multi-agent systems remain unreliable
**Mitigation:** Use fewer, tightly scoped reviewers. Add deterministic tools and human approval. Measure reliability; do not sell autonomy.

### Risk: incumbents bundle decision logs
**Mitigation:** Own the technical review workflow and integrations. Be the specialised layer, not a generic governance suite.

### Risk: buyers will not pay for reviews
**Mitigation:** Sell to teams with expensive decisions and existing review budgets. Test per-review pricing with consultancies and agencies.

### Risk: the market is smaller than the forecasts suggest
**Mitigation:** Keep the cost base low. Win a narrow niche before expanding. A $5M ARR specialised business is still valuable.

### Kill or pivot criteria
- **After 20 design partners:** fewer than 3 run a second review → the wedge is wrong.
- **After 5 paid pilots:** fewer than 2 convert → pricing or value is wrong.
- **After the benchmark:** council does not beat a single model by a meaningful margin → drop “council” as the core story and reposition around evidence and review.
- **After 6 months:** cost per review exceeds 30% of price → fix routing, caching, and model choice.
- **After 12 months:** no team invites a second person → this is a single-player tool, not a team product; change the model or the buyer.

## 13. Operating principles

1. **Sell the record, not the agents.**
2. **Proof beats demos.**
3. **One wedge, then expand.**
4. **Integrate where the decision already happens.**
5. **Private by default. Public by choice.**
6. **Human approval is a feature, not a fallback.**
7. **Measure outcomes, not activity.**
8. **Model-agnostic; workflow-obsessed.**
9. **Cut features that do not improve review quality or time-to-decision.**
10. **If the council does not add evidence, dissent, or accountability, remove it.**
